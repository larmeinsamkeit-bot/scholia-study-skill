## 1. Opportunity cost

The opportunity cost of an item is what you give up to get it: the value of the **next best
alternative**, not the sum of all alternatives.

Example: a student can either work a shift paying $120 or attend a review session. The opportunity
cost of the session is $120 (plus anything else the next best alternative would have provided).

Sunk costs — costs already paid that cannot be recovered — are **not** part of opportunity cost.

## 2. The production possibilities frontier (PPF)

A PPF shows the combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its resources and technology.

- Points **on** the frontier are efficient.
- Points **inside** are inefficient (unemployed resources).
- Points **outside** are unattainable for now.

The slope of the PPF is the opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis, measured in units of
the good on the vertical axis.

Worked example: Farmland can produce either 100 tonnes of wheat or 50 tonnes of rice, at a constant
rate. The opportunity cost of 1 tonne of rice is 100 / 50 = 2 tonnes of wheat.

A bowed-out (concave) PPF means increasing opportunity cost, because resources are not equally suited
to producing both goods.

## 3. Comparative advantage

A producer has a **comparative advantage** in a good if it has the lower opportunity cost of producing
it. Absolute advantage (producing more with the same inputs) is not what drives trade.

Example: In one hour, Ann makes 4 shirts or 2 hats; Ben makes 1 shirt or 1 hat.
Ann's opportunity cost of a hat = 2 shirts; Ben's = 1 shirt. Ben has the comparative advantage in hats
even though Ann has the absolute advantage in both.
